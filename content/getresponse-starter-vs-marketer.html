The decision

Choose Starter until a specific campaign needs Marketer.

Starter is the sensible starting point for a small business sending newsletters, collecting subscribers, and running a straightforward welcome series. Choose Marketer when you need several separate automation workflows, tagging or scoring in those workflows, or ecommerce follow-up such as abandoned cart recovery. A small list can need Marketer; a larger newsletter list can still suit Starter.

The detail that changes the decision is Starter’s automation allowance: one custom workflow containing at most six elements. A trigger, a message, and a waiting step each use part of that budget. It is not permission to build one enormous automation containing every campaign.

For the entry tier of 1,000 contacts, monthly billing starts at $19 for Starter and $59 for Marketer. Before spending the extra $40 a month, name the campaign that needs it. If your answer is simply “send a useful email every week,” start with Starter.

List size and feature level are separate choices

The standard contact bands are 1,000, 2,500, 5,000, 10,000, 25,000, 50,000, and 100,000. Moving above 1,000 contacts does not inherently mean you need Marketer. You may need a higher contact allowance on Starter. Obtain the current quote for that allowance instead of extending the entry price to a larger list.

Count list memberships carefully. For example, 650 people copied into each of three lists represent 1,950 contact records, not 650 billable contacts. GetResponse counts an address separately on each list. Its billing guidance also considers the peak active count and contacts added during the billing month; deleting contacts later does not necessarily reverse the month’s charge.

03 / Count every element

A complete welcome sequence that fits Starter’s six-element budget.

Imagine a local garden-maintenance business offering a seasonal care guide. It wants to deliver the guide, share one practical tip, and invite the subscriber to request a quote. Here is a proposed custom workflow using six basic elements.

Original planning example: one entry condition, three messages, and two waits.
ElementConfigurationBusiness purpose
1. Subscribed viaSelect the intended guide signup list and subscription methodStart the right subscribers on this journey
2. Send messageGuide delivery emailFulfill the signup promise
3. WaitTwo daysLeave time to read the guide
4. Send messageOne seasonal mistake and how to avoid itProvide a useful follow-up
5. WaitThree daysSpace the next message
6. Send messageExplain the service and link to the quote request pageOffer a clear next step

Connect the steps in that order. The timing is relative: the last email follows the second wait, rather than being scheduled for a universal calendar date. This design assumes the entry list contains subscribers who signed up for this marketing sequence. It has no spare element for an additional consent branch, interest tag, or purchase check.

GetResponse documents the two-element Simple welcome message template using Subscribed via and Send message. Its Wait action supplies a delay before the next action. This expanded design uses those documented building blocks; it is not a claim that we published it in a paid account.

Sources: Simple welcome message, Wait action, and basic automation on Starter.

What would make this design outgrow Starter?

Adding a fourth message after another wait would take this particular design to eight elements. A separate second workflow would exceed the workflow allowance even if both workflows were short. Adding a tag is also outside Starter’s available automation features; deleting a wait to stay below six does not unlock it.

Before upgrading, ask whether the additional step changes the customer’s experience enough to justify the complexity. If you only want a longer sequence on fixed subscription days, check autoresponders next.

04 / Use the simpler option

A longer scheduled email series may not need Marketer.

Starter also includes autoresponders. These send messages on selected days of a contact’s subscription cycle: for example, an introduction on day 0, a useful lesson on day 3, and an invitation on day 7. They are separate from the visual workflow described above.

A useful distinction is “send this after a certain number of days” versus “send this because this person did something.” A fixed educational series is a good candidate for autoresponders. Routing people according to purchases, assigning interest tags, or coordinating several separate journeys points toward Marketer.

Do not conclude that Starter limits your entire account to three automated emails just because the sample workflow contains three sends. Its six-element cap applies to that custom workflow. For a longer time-based series, first check whether the autoresponder cycle already does the job.

For a complete example, follow our GetResponse PDF lead-magnet tutorial with three finished emails.

When setting up a cycle, confirm the subscription method adds the contact to the intended day. A contact entering on day 1 can miss a message assigned to day 0. Use a fresh test subscription before directing customers to the form, and avoid activating duplicate welcome emails in both systems.

05 / Put the upgrade to work

Three small-business decisions, with the costs made explicit.

A service business with 650 contacts: start with Starter

Suppose the garden-maintenance business has one signup list, one welcome series, and a monthly seasonal newsletter. At the entry allowance, Starter costs $19 a month or a calculated $186.96 prepaid year. The sample workflow fits, and the newsletter does not need its own custom automation.

Marketer would cost $480 more across twelve monthly payments. Spend that only when a new requirement earns a place in the plan. Improving the quote page, writing the emails, and answering inquiries may be more useful first steps than adding automated branches.

A small shop with 850 contacts: consider Marketer for a real recovery process

Suppose the shop needs a welcome journey and separate abandoned cart follow-up, with purchase information determining what happens next. Marketer’s ecommerce capabilities and multiple workflows make it the relevant tier to evaluate. But first check whether the shop platform already provides the recovery messages you need. Paying two tools to send overlapping reminders can create work instead of removing it.

At monthly entry pricing, the incremental fee is $40. If an additional completed order contributes $20 after product, fulfillment, payment, and discount costs, two genuinely additional orders cover that fee. Two orders merely attributed to an email are not automatically two additional orders. This is break-even arithmetic, not a prediction that cart recovery will produce them.

Our Shopify abandoned-cart setup guide shows the integration and purchase tests to complete first.

Test the store connection, product data, and purchase signals before you depend on the campaign. A plan entitlement does not establish that a particular store’s integration is configured correctly.

A business with 2,400 newsletter subscribers: increase the allowance, not automatically the plan

A larger audience does not turn a newsletter into a complex automation program. If the work remains newsletters plus scheduled education, compare Starter at 2,500 contacts with Marketer at the same allowance. Do not compare the larger Starter quote with the entry Marketer headline.

Count duplicate list memberships and expected signups before choosing the allowance. Crossing the limit can create extension fees; the current overage documentation explains how imports and other subscription methods are handled.

Sources for these scenarios: plan capabilities and list sizes; list extension rules. Business circumstances, contribution margins, and break-even calculations are original illustrations.

06 / Test the intended paid plan

Do not mistake a successful trial workflow for Starter compatibility.

GetResponse’s first 14 days expose premium features subject to trial limits. Its trial help page allows publishing multiple workflows. Consequently, building two workflows during the trial does not prove you can keep both on Starter. A template can also be visible without being publishable on your eventual plan.

Sources: trial capabilities and restrictions; viewing versus publishing templates.

  1. Write one campaign brief. State who enters, what they receive, what ends the journey, and which customer action changes the next message.
  2. Count workflows and elements. Include entry conditions, waits, filters, and actions. Mark any tag, score, or ecommerce requirement.
  3. Price the actual contact allowance. Include overlapping lists and realistic near-term growth.
  4. Test the complete route. Use a fresh subscription, check the promised email and links, and verify the intended follow-up. For a timing test, shorten delays deliberately and restore the production values afterward.
  5. Match every required feature to the paid plan. Record which features were available only because the account was in its trial period. Choose the lowest plan that supports the finished route.

Keep monthly billing while the setup is unsettled. Annual billing becomes a clearer decision once you know which workflows you will actually maintain.

07 / Make the purchase decision

Upgrade for a named constraint, and stay put when it disappears.

Marketer is worth evaluating when you can finish this sentence: “We need to run [specific campaign], and Starter cannot support [specific element, capability, or additional workflow].” Then assign an owner, define the outcome to measure, and compare that expected value with the incremental cost and setup time.

Stay on Starter when your emails mainly educate everyone on the same schedule, your single workflow fits, and you do not need the gated features. Consider another platform if your existing system already handles the necessary campaigns well, migration costs outweigh the benefit, or GetResponse’s price at your actual contact count exceeds your budget. Avoid buying Marketer solely because it carries the “most popular” label.

If you later change plans, GetResponse says upgrades take effect immediately, restart the billing cycle, and credit unused subscription time against the new charge. Its subscription-change help page directs downgrades through support. Review the displayed amount and timing before changing an annual commitment.

GetResponse Starter vs Marketer FAQ

Is Starter enough for a small business?

Often, yes. The deciding factor is what your campaigns must do. Newsletters, forms, and time-based education can fit Starter. Identify a feature it cannot provide before assuming that business growth requires Marketer.

Can Starter run a welcome email sequence?

Yes. Use autoresponders for a subscription-day sequence, or a custom workflow within the six-element allowance. The worked example above uses one entry condition, three messages, and two waits. It leaves no room for an extra branch.

Does Marketer include webinars?

Not as an included feature in the current standard plan comparison. Creator includes webinars. Some help pages discuss add-ons, but availability can depend on the account and region; do not purchase Marketer assuming webinars are bundled.

Sources: current plan inclusions; webinar add-on and regional restrictions.

Can I continue automations free after the trial?

Do not plan on that. The free account remains available, but premium access ends after 14 days. The trial help page says post-trial free users cannot send autoresponder or automation messages until upgrading. Free access, a premium trial, and paid Starter are three different sets of capabilities.

Source: what happens after the trial.

Should I buy the annual plan immediately?

Only if you have verified the workflow, contact allowance, and commitment. Annual pricing lowers the equivalent monthly rate, but it asks you to pay for a year before you have used it. A short period of monthly billing can be worthwhile while you establish that the plan fits.
